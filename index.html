Ask what divides BC voters and most people answer: money. The data says otherwise. Match the 2021 Census against four elections and the real dividing lines are degrees, deeds and grey hair — education, homeownership and age. Income barely registers.

01 · The riddleIncome tells you almost nothing

correlation, median household income × NDP share 2024

Start with the oldest assumption in politics: parties of the left win the poor, parties of the right win the rich. In today's BC it simply isn't true. Across all 93 ridings, median household income has no relationship with the NDP vote — the correlation is statistically indistinguishable from zero, and it was just as dead in 2017.

Each dot is a riding, coloured by its 2024 winner. The line is flat. Sort ridings into five income tiers and the NDP's share barely moves across them.

02 · DegreesThe diploma divide — and it's widening

%. bachelor's+ × NDP share, 2024
same correlation in 2017

What income can't explain, education does. The share of 25-to-64-year-olds holding a university degree is now the strongest demographic predictor of the NDP vote — and its grip doubled between 2017 and 2024. Sort ridings into five education tiers: the NDP's 2024 share climbs a -point staircase from the least-degreed fifth to the most.

In 2017 that staircase was shallow and crooked. The sorting of BC politics by education is new, fast, and — on this evidence — still accelerating.

03 · DeedsA tenants' party, a landlords' opposition

% homeowner households × NDP share, 2024

The second great sorter is the property line. The higher a riding's share of owner households, the lower its NDP vote — and in the full statistical model, tenure is the single most robust predictor, stronger than education once everything else is controlled. The NDP is, structurally, the party of renters.

This isn't a wealth effect in disguise: dwelling values run the other way (pricier ridings lean NDP, because they're urban), and income does nothing. It's tenure itself.

04 · LanguageThe multilingual city leans NDP…

% non-official home language × NDP share, 2024

Now the map turns to who BC's ridings are. Across all 93 ridings, the more residents who speak a language other than English or French at home, the higher the NDP's 2024 share ran — as a level, linguistic diversity tilts orange. But the tilt is no longer a lock: of the ten most multilingual ridings — where a third to a half of households live in another language — the NDP won just in 2024.

Levels and movement are different things. Hold that thought.

05 · The swing…but it's exactly where the NDP bled

% non-official language × NDP swing 2020→2024, metro ridings

Compare 2024 against the 2020 result restated on today's boundaries and the pattern inverts. Within Metro Vancouver, the more multilingual the riding, the harder the NDP fell — this is Surrey, Richmond and east Vancouver moving as one. Meanwhile the party gained in the degree-rich, high-home-value west side and North Shore.

One realignment, two faces: the NDP traded ground in immigrant suburbs for ground in educated enclaves. The 2020 baseline here is notional (2020 votes restated onto 2024 boundaries), so read the pattern, not any single riding's decimal.

06 · Grey powerThe electorate that shows up is old

Turn from how ridings vote to who votes at all. Elections BC counts participation by age, so this is measured, not estimated: in every election since 2005, turnout climbs with age. 07 · The turnout machineTurnout is demographics, almost entirely

Riding turnout in 2024 ranged from % in to % in — and almost none of that spread is mystery. Age pushes turnout up; linguistic diversity — a proxy for newer, busier, less-courted electorates — pushes it down harder than anything else. Seven census variables explain three-quarters of all variation.

08 · The strategic bindThe majority rests on the emptiest polls

mean turnout in the NDP's 8 narrowest holds
province-wide turnout, 2024

Put the halves together and the bind sharpens. Averaged over all seats, turnout barely differs by winner — NDP-won and Conservative-won ridings both land near 58 per cent, because the party's high-turnout Island strongholds offset its low-turnout suburbs. The bind is inside the NDP coalition: the knife-edge seats its majority actually stands on run three points below the provincial average, and its five Surrey-and-city holds among them sit at the very bottom of the table — in exactly the young, multilingual, renter-heavy places this story has mapped.

A one-seat majority defended in the province's lowest-turnout ridings cuts both ways: it is the party's biggest exposure, and — because every non-voter there is a knock on a door away — its cheapest insurance.

09 · MethodWhat this can and can't say

Everything here is an ecological analysis: it describes ridings, not individuals. "Renter-heavy ridings vote NDP" is not proof that renters do — though the consistency of these gradients across four elections, two boundary sets and two independent linkage methods makes the patterns hard to dismiss.

Sources & Method

Demographics. 2021 Census of Population profile by Forward Sortation Area (Statistics Canada, 98-401-X2021013). FSA values were allocated to ridings two independent ways: (a) counting each riding's postal codes per FSA (Elections BC postal-code translation, 2017 boundaries), and (b) spatially assigning each of 5,778 voting-area polygons to its FSA and weighting by registered voters (2024 boundaries). On ridings unchanged between boundary sets the two methods agree with r = 0.94–0.99; riding "medians" are population-weighted means of FSA medians, so treat them as estimates.

Results & turnout. Elections BC official results and registered-voter counts by voting area and riding (2013, 2017, 2020, 2024); participation by age group as published. 2024 associations use the 93 current ridings; 2017/2020 associations use the 87 ridings of that era natively. The 2020→2024 swing restates 2020 votes on 2024 boundaries via Elections BC's voting-area correspondence — vote totals are conserved exactly; the split of at-large ballots is proportional and notional.

Statistics. Pearson correlations (n=93 or 87); multivariate results are standardized OLS. Correlation is not causation; riding-level association is not individual behaviour. 2013 riding demographics aren't computable from the available crosswalks (its 85-seat boundaries predate them), so 2013 enters only through province-wide turnout-by-age and the historical NDP-share record.

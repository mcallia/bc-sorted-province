The Fall of
Fortress Seymour

Seymour was the North Shore's Liberal citadel — nearly 60 per cent of the vote in 2009, still standing in 2017 when Lonsdale next door fell. It finally broke in 2020. What happened in 2024 is stranger: while the province swung right, this wealthy, house-proud riding kept marching the other way — and its old Liberal heartland marched hardest.

01 · The groundSeventy polls, from Lynn Valley to Deep Cove

Seymour is the North Shore's eastern half: Lynn Valley's family streets, the Tsleil-Waututh Nation's shoreline, Blueridge and Parkgate under the mountain, out to the cottages of Deep Cove. Seventy voting areas, thirteen election-day voting places, about 46,000 registered voters.

On paper this is exactly the ground the right should hold: 77 per cent of households own their home — the highest tenure on the North Shore — the average dwelling is worth $1.56 million, and the median household earns $124,000. But half its adults hold a university degree, and in the sorted province of 2024, that turned out to matter more.

02 · The arcThe fortress held — then it didn't

For three elections this seat wasn't close: the BC Liberals took 56.9, 59.2 and 50.9 per cent. In 2017, the year Bowinn Ma flipped Lonsdale, Seymour held — 46.4 to the NDP's 34.5. It took the 2020 landslide for Susie Chant to break through at 46.8. Then 2024: the province lurched right, and Seymour went to 52.8 per cent NDP — a four-way race against a Conservative at 35.8, with a Green and independent Mitchell Baker splitting the rest.

Chant's margin: 5,215 votes. The party that owned this seat for a generation is gone from the ballot.

04 · The swingSixty-nine of seventy — and one lonely holdout

Compare each poll's election-day vote in 2017 — when the fortress still held — with 2024. Sixty-nine of seventy polls swung toward the NDP, by an average of 15.7 points. And the pattern inverts the old map: the hardest swings came off the deepest Liberal ground — polls around Parkgate and upper Lonsdale where the NDP started as low as 22 per cent moved by up to +22.8.

The lone holdout is poll NVS059 in Lynnmour — down 1.7 points. The irony: it arrived from Lonsdale in the redistribution, and it was already the most NDP-friendly ground in the riding. The only place Seymour got bluer was the place with the least blue to lose.

05 · The boundaryEight polls came over from Lonsdale — the seat was won without them

The 2023 redistribution moved eight polls from Lonsdale into Seymour — highlighted here. They are friendlier ground for the NDP (their election-day share runs — per cent, against — in the sixty-two native polls). But the arithmetic is not close: the flip happened in 2020 on the old boundaries, and in 2024 the native sixty-two swung just as decisively as the newcomers.

Redistribution shuffled the deck. It didn't deal the hand.

06 · Every doorFour ways to vote, one result

As in Lonsdale, there is no channel the Conservatives can point to. The NDP won the advance vote 54.3 to 35.1, election day 51.0 to 36.4, and the mail vote 60.7 to 31.1 — with the Green and independent votes flat across all of them. However the ballots arrived, the Conservatives never left the thirties.

07 · Who shows upThe North Shore's turnout engine

Seymour votes like it owns the place — because it does. Turnout was 66.6 per cent in 2024, nearly eight points above Lonsdale, and its age curve is the classic homeowner gradient: 75.3 per cent among 65-to-74s against 60.0 for the youngest voters. This is the profile that is supposed to protect incumbent conservatism — older, propertied, high-participation. In 2024 it delivered a 17-point NDP win instead.

08 · What Seymour meansWhere the diploma beat the deed

In The Sorted Province we found BC's two strongest structural pulls working against each other here: education pushes a riding toward the NDP, home-ownership pulls it right. Seymour is where they fought it out — 77 per cent owners, half with degrees — and education won, twice over: first the flip in 2020, then a counter-swing in 2024 that ran hardest through the riding's most affluent, most propertied polls.

Lonsdale shows the realignment's new heartland. Seymour shows its frontier — and how far it has already advanced.

Sources & Method

Results. Elections BC official results. Riding-level figures 2005–2024 are actual counts across all voting opportunities. Poll-level maps use the only sub-riding data published: general voting by voting area (2017) and final voting by voting place (2024, attributed to voting-area catchments via Elections BC's official VA→voting-place assignment).

The swing map compares in-person election-day universes only (59% of 2017 votes; 41% of 2024). Both years' advance and absentee ballots are reported at riding level only. Polls altered by the 2023 redistribution use registered-voter-weighted estimates from their 2017 parent polls, per Elections BC's conversion table, and are tagged estimated in tooltips. Eight polls transferred in from North Vancouver-Lonsdale carry their Lonsdale 2017 baselines. Six site-based voting areas (care facilities and similar) have no map geography and are excluded from the poll count.

Demographics: 2021 Census (Statistics Canada), FSA profiles weighted to the riding by registered voters per voting area. Turnout: ballots cast ÷ registered voters, Elections BC. Age participation: Elections BC voter participation by age group.

These are area-level associations; they do not establish individual behaviour.
